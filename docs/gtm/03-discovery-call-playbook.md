# 03 · Discovery-call playbook — the operator's manual for one call

> Governed by `docs/gtm/00-content-brief.md` and `docs/VOICE.md`. The strategy,
> the offer, and the question *set* live in `docs/design-partner-kit.md` and
> `discovery/interview-guide.md`. **This file does not repeat them — it is the
> minute-by-minute how-to for running a single call well.** Read the kit first;
> this is the tactical layer on top of it.

**The one rule that governs the whole call:** you are learning, not selling. Talk
under 30% of the time. Never lead the witness toward the AI-disclosure moat — ask
open questions and let them raise it. You are a **builder who wants to be told
what's wrong**, not a vendor closing a deal. Every claim obeys the honesty rules:
the tool **structures the expert's own findings**, restricts the model to supplied
evidence, and checks missing or unknown citation IDs; the expert verifies support;
admissibility is the court's call; the record is **tamper-evident**, not
tamper-proof; no certs we don't hold.

---

## 1. The 24-hours-before prep checklist

Do this the day before, not five minutes before. A respected expert can tell in
the first minute whether you did your homework.

**Research the person (15 min):**
- [ ] Read their directory listing / CV / LinkedIn. Note their **discipline and
      sub-specialty** (e.g. vocational rehab → earning capacity vs. life-care
      planning), years in practice, and whether they're **solo or in a firm**.
- [ ] Find **one specific, true detail** to open with — a talk they gave, a
      publication, the directory they're listed in, a mutual contact. Write it
      down verbatim so you don't fumble it live.
- [ ] Note their likely **methodology and standard** so you can follow along, not
      so you can lecture: vocational rehab → **RAPEL (Weed)**, TSA coded to the
      **DOT/SVP**, tools like **OASYS/SkillTRAN**, **O*NET**, **BLS** wages.
      Accident recon → **IPTM/ACTAR**. Engineering → **ASTM E3176/E620**.
- [ ] Re-read the founder posture below so the "are you an expert?" question
      doesn't catch you flat-footed.

**Have the materials staged (5 min):**
- [ ] **Sample report ready in a tab** — the discipline-matched one if it exists
      (`/sample`), plus the **PDF/DOCX export** open so you can show the
      AI-Disclosure Appendix as a real artifact, not a promise.
- [ ] **Demo staged but not center-stage** — `/intake` open in a tab in case they
      want to paste a paragraph and watch it ground + disclose. Do not plan to
      drive it; plan to offer it only if they pull.
- [ ] **Resources article** open (`/resources/ai-disclosure-in-expert-reports`)
      in case they want the case-law write-up after the call.
- [ ] Capture sheet / post-call template (§8 below) open in a separate window.

**Set the logistics:**
- [ ] Send the **sample link the day before** so they can glance at it cold; the
      call is then a conversation about something real.
- [ ] Confirm you have **20–30 min**, not 60 — a tight call respects their rate.
- [ ] Silence notifications. One screen for them, one for your notes.

## 2. The opening 60 seconds — set the frame as learning, not selling

The first minute decides whether they relax or brace. Your job: take selling off
the table out loud, name the one true personal detail, and hand them the floor.

**The exact words (adapt, don't recite robotically):**

> "Thanks for making the time — I know what your hour's worth, so I'll keep it
> tight. There's nothing to buy on this call and I'm not going to pitch you. I'm
> building a reporting tool for [discipline] experts, and I'd rather be told what's
> wrong with it by someone who actually writes these than keep guessing. So mostly
> I want to ask you questions. That work?"

Then the **one specific detail**, immediately:

> "I saw [you spoke on X at the IARP section / your listing on SEAK / Jane
> connected us] — that's actually why I wanted you specifically."

Then **hand over the floor** and stop talking:

> "Can I start with how report writing actually works for you?"

**Why this frame works:** "nothing to buy" + "tell me what's wrong" disarms a
skeptic trained to find the overstatement. It makes the expert the authority and
you the student — which is also the honest truth of the relationship. It earns the
candor that a sales frame kills.

**Frame-setting do/don't:**
- **Do** say "I'm trying not to build the wrong thing." It's honest and it works.
- **Don't** open with the product, a slide, or the case law. No "as you may know,
  courts are now…". You lecture last, if at all.
- **Don't** apologize for their time more than once — it reads as unsure.

## 3. The question flow — with listening cues and strong/weak answers

The **questions come from the kit** (`design-partner-kit.md` §5 and
`interview-guide.md`). Don't re-ask them off a fresh list — work the kit's flow.
What's below is the **tactical layer**: the active-listening cue for each block,
the follow-up probe that gets the real answer, and how to tell a strong signal
from a weak one. Anchor everything on their **most recent actual report**, never a
hypothetical.

**Active-listening rules that run under every block:**
- After they answer, **stay silent 2–3 seconds.** The best detail comes in the
  pause you're tempted to fill.
- **Reflect, then probe:** "So the writing itself ate most of a day — did I get
  that right?" then "What part specifically?" Mirroring earns more than a new
  question.
- **Echo their exact words** in your notes and back to them. If they say
  "soul-crushing," you say "the soul-crushing part" later — not "the tedious part."
- **Chase numbers and specifics:** hours, reports/year, dollars. Vague pain is not
  validation. "Walk me through *that* one" beats "in general."
- **Never correct them**, never finish their sentence, never defend the product
  mid-answer. You are collecting, not persuading.

---

### Block A — The work & the pain *(the core; ~8 min)*

**Kit questions:** last report start-to-finish; where the time goes; what they
dread/write off; reports per year; format/standard; what reviewers nitpick.

**Listening cue:** Listen for whether the pain is **drafting/structuring** (your
lane) or **investigation/analysis** (not your lane). If the bottleneck is the
analysis, not the writing, that's a real finding — write it down, don't argue it.

**Best follow-up probes:**
- "Where does the time actually go — is it the analysis, or getting it onto the
  page in the right form?"
- "Do you **bill** that writing time, write it off, or eat it?" *(If they eat it,
  the pain is personal lost time — the strongest signal.)*
- "Last time a report went out late or sloppy — what caused it?"
- "What do opposing counsel or reviewers nitpick most?" *(maps to Daubert/702
  failure modes — listen for data→opinion path gaps, generic job titles, weak
  labor-market-survey method.)*

| | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong answer** | A concrete number ("3–4 hours, and I don't bill it"), a named dread ("the TSA boilerplate / the qualifications section"), a real recent failure, and a clear standard they work to. The pain is in **structuring and assembling**, which is your lane. |
| **Weak answer** | "It's fine, I have a template that works." / "The hard part is the analysis, the writing's quick." / Can't quote a number. Vague or low pain = weak fit. Note it honestly; don't talk them into a problem. |

---

### Block B — Tools today *(~3 min)*

**Kit questions:** what they write in; whether they've tried any AI; what happened.

**Listening cue:** Listen for the **rebuke or the fear** when AI comes up — that's
the moat surfacing on its own. Do **not** prompt it. If they volunteer worry about
hallucinated cites or disclosure, you've struck the vein; let them keep going.

**Best follow-up probes:**
- "What happened when you tried it?" *(let the war story run)*
- "If a junior could assemble the boilerplate sections from your findings, leaving
  you to write the opinions — would that help, or is the boilerplate not the
  bottleneck?" *(the kill-criterion question — a genuine "boilerplate isn't my
  problem" is a real no.)*

| | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong answer** | "I tried ChatGPT once, it invented a citation, never again." / "I'd use AI if I could trust it and account for it." → unprompted moat resonance. |
| **Weak answer** | "Haven't thought about it, don't care." + boilerplate isn't their bottleneck → the kill-criterion is tripping; this segment may be weak. |

---

### Block C — AI & disclosure *(the moat test; ~5 min)*

**Kit questions:** AI in practice today + worries; whether disclosure has come up
in their matters/association; how they'd answer a judge asking how they used AI.

**Listening cue:** The whole test is **does this matter to them *unprompted*.**
The instant you explain why disclosure is valuable, the signal is contaminated.
Ask the open question, then **shut up** and grade whether they reach for it
themselves.

**Best follow-up probes:**
- "Has the AI-disclosure question come up — in a matter, or in your association?"
- "If opposing counsel asked in a depo *how* you used AI on a report, how would you
  answer today?" *(listen for discomfort — that discomfort is the product's
  reason to exist.)*
- Only **if they ask** what you mean, reference the moment factually and briefly:
  a declaration was struck in *Kohls v. Ellison* over fabricated cites; in *CLF v.
  Shell* a magistrate ordered an expert to produce her AI prompts — **and note
  that last one is a non-final order under review.** Then return the floor. This is
  context on request, not a pitch.

| | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong answer** | They raise disclosure/Daubert anxiety *before you do*: "I'd be nervous in a depo," "our section is talking about this." The moat resonates unprompted. |
| **Weak answer** | Shrug. "Nobody's asked, I wouldn't worry." Combined with low writing pain, this is the documented NO-GO signal — record it straight. |

---

### Block D — Money & trust *(~3 min)*

**Kit questions:** what reclaiming [X hours] with them still as author is worth,
per report or subscription; what would make them *not* trust it.

**Listening cue:** Don't flinch at a low number and don't argue it. The number
**is the data.** And the trust block often surfaces the real #1 objection — write
it down word-for-word; it feeds `04-objection-handling.md`.

**Best follow-up probes:**
- "Per report, or a subscription — which fits how you actually work?" *(settles
  per-report vs. seat; tie to their reports/year from Block A.)*
- "What would have to be true for you to trust it on a report under your
  signature?" *(the liability bar — the deal-breakers.)*

**Pricing validation — get these three answered (the calls set the price, not a spreadsheet):**
1. *"How many reports do you write in a typical year?"* — the number that decides per-report vs. annual. (Confirm against Block A.)
2. *"For a tool that does the structuring and keeps the disclosure record — with you still authoring every word — what would you pay per report?"* — let them name it first; don't anchor.
3. *"Per report, or one annual flat rate — and at what yearly number does the annual become an obvious yes?"* — settles the hero tier and what to charge for it.

Log all three in the five answers (§8). Across ~8–10 calls the *distribution* — not any single reply — is what sets the price.

| | Sounds like |
| --- | --- |
| **Strong answer** | A real number tied to their hourly rate, a clear pricing preference, and a **specific, satisfiable** trust condition ("I'd have to review every line" — which is exactly the design). |
| **Weak answer** | "Wouldn't pay for it." / Trust bar is "I'd never trust software with this" with no condition that could change it. |

## 4. Showing the sample/demo WITHOUT pitching

The sample is the strongest asset you have, *because* it lets the artifact talk
instead of you. Showing it is still not pitching — it's handing them something to
critique.

**When to show it:**
- Best: **before the call** (link in the day-before email), so the call is a
  reaction, not a reveal.
- On the call: only **after** you've mined the pain (end of Block C / start of
  Block D), and frame it as a **request for critique**, not a demo:
  > "Can I show you two minutes of a sample and have you tell me what's wrong with
  > it? I want the holes, not a compliment."
- Show **two things and stop:** (1) the body with a couple of cited sentences;
  (2) the **AI-Disclosure Appendix** in the export — "this records each AI-assisted
  section, the model and version, and exactly the evidence it was given." Then ask
  "what would your version need?" and go quiet.

**The demo (`/intake`) — only on pull:** if they say "wait, how does it know not
to make something up?", *then* offer: "Want to paste a sentence and watch it? If
the source isn't there, it flags the gap instead of filling it." Let **them**
drive. One honest behavior beats a scripted tour.

**When NOT to show anything:**
- If the pain isn't there yet — showing the product to fill silence flips you into
  sales mode and the candor stops.
- If they're already skeptical of "AI for reports" — sit in the objection and
  listen first; a demo on top of an unaddressed fear confirms the fear.
- If you're running low on time — protect the **five answers** (§8) over the demo.
- **Never** narrate it with outcome claims: not "this makes your report
  admissible / court-defensible / compliant." Say what it **does** — structures,
  cites to supplied evidence, flags gaps, records the AI use. The verb is the
  product's integrity; if you overclaim here you lose the room.

## 5. "Are you a vocational-rehab expert?" — the honest builder posture

It will come up. The honest answer is also the credible one. A real expert smells
a faked credential instantly, and faking one is how you lose a whole referral
community.

**Say, plainly:**

> "No — I'm not a [vocational-rehab] expert, and I'm not going to pretend to be.
> You're the expert; that's the whole reason I want to build this *with* people
> like you. What I do understand is the **AI-disclosure problem** — how to keep an
> honest, contemporaneous record of how AI was used — and I've built the tool so
> it **structures your findings and never puts words in your mouth.** The
> methodology has to come from you. The template I've drafted is desk research
> until someone like you red-teams it."

**Why this works:** it converts the gap into the offer. You're not selling
expertise you don't have — you're offering a tool plus the willingness to listen,
and asking them to be the authority. It's disarming *because* it's true, and it
sets up the design-partner ask. **Lead with the AI-disclosure problem (which you
genuinely understand); let them be the expert on their discipline.**

**Do not:** bluff a RAPEL/DOT/ASTM detail to sound credentialed. If you don't
know, "I don't — teach me" outperforms a wrong answer every time.

## 6. Reading the signals — buy / no-buy / polite-no

You're reading for **honest fit**, not a close. A real no is more valuable than a
soft maybe, because it saves you both time and protects your name in a referral
net.

**Buy / strong-fit signals (lean into the design-partner ask):**
- Unprompted pain with a **number** and an unbilled-time admission.
- Raises **disclosure/Daubert anxiety before you do.**
- Asks **forward-looking questions:** "when could I use it on a real one?",
  "what's it cost?", "could it do my ASTM format?"
- Offers a **redacted sample** of their own work, or starts critiquing yours in
  detail — investment behavior.
- Volunteers a **peer's name** before you ask.

**No-buy / weak-fit signals (don't push — record and thank them):**
- Boilerplate isn't the bottleneck **and** disclosure doesn't worry them (the
  documented NO-GO combination).
- Can't or won't quote any hours or dollars.
- "Software can't do what I do" with **no condition** that would change their mind.

**The "polite no" — learn to hear it (and respect it):**
- "This is great, send me more info." / "Let me think about it." / "Circle back
  after [distant event]." / enthusiasm with **zero specific question.**
- **Do not** chase with a pitch. Convert it into the one thing it can still give
  you — a referral and a reason:
  > "Totally fair. Two quick things and I'll let you go: is it that the timing's
  > off, or that the problem isn't sharp for you? And — who's one person who writes
  > these *really* well that I should learn from?"
- A polite no that yields an honest reason + a name is a **successful call.** Log
  it as a no, not a maybe; soft maybes rot the pipeline.

## 7. The design-partner ask, "free, for good," and the referral

Make the ask **once**, plainly, near the end — only if signals warrant. If they
don't, skip straight to the referral; a forced ask after a no costs you the intro.

**The design-partner ask (the exact frame):**

> "Here's the only thing I'd ask. I'm taking on **two or three [discipline]
> experts** to shape the template with me — **free, for as long as you'll have
> it.** In exchange: a couple of short calls, and you red-team one real,
> de-identified report against your own standard so I know it holds up. You'd have
> a direct say in how your discipline's template works. Would you want one of those
> slots?"

**How to frame "free, for good" honestly:**
- It's **free because they're doing the work** of making it real — not a discount,
  a **value exchange.** Say that; it lands better and it's true.
- Be straight on what you ask: 2–3 calls, one red-team, optional intros. **Never**
  ask for their **case data under protective order**, money, or a **public
  endorsement before they believe in it.**
- **Professional responsibility for any real report stays entirely with them** —
  the tool structures supplied findings, checks citation IDs, and leaves
  substantive source verification, preparation, adoption, and signature to the
  expert. Saying this out loud is reassurance *and* it's the product.
- Don't oversell the "founding advisor" framing; offer recognition only **if they
  want it.** Some experts won't want their name on a pre-launch tool — respect it.

**The referral ask (do this on *every* call, fit or not):**

> "Last thing — who else writes these well that I should be learning from? Even one
> name helps more than you'd think."
- Ask **after** the partner ask on a strong call; **instead of** it on a weak one.
- If they offer a name, ask the multiplier: **"Mind if I say you pointed me to
  them?"** A warm intro outperforms 50 cold messages in this community.

## 8. After every call — the five answers + post-call template

**Fill this within 10 minutes, while their words are fresh.** These five answers
*are* the validation — the call didn't happen until they're written down. (Same
five as the kit; this is the capture form.)

**The five to capture every time:**
1. **Time / pain — in their words** (the exact phrase, with the hours).
2. **Volume per year** (settles per-report vs. seat pricing).
3. **Willingness-to-pay number** (per report and/or per month).
4. **Did AI-disclosure matter *unprompted*?** (Y/N + the quote if yes.)
5. **The #1 objection** (verbatim — this feeds `04-objection-handling.md`).

## 9. The one-screen cheat sheet

- **Frame:** "Nothing to buy. Tell me what's wrong with it." Talk <30%.
- **Open:** thanks + one true detail + hand them the floor.
- **Listen:** silence after answers; mirror their words; chase numbers.
- **Don't lead the moat:** ask open; let them raise disclosure.
- **Sample:** only after the pain, only as "what's wrong with this?" Never claim
  admissible/compliant/court-defensible. The verb is **structures**.
- **"Are you the expert?":** No — and that's the offer. Lead with the disclosure
  problem; let them be the expert.
- **Signals:** forward questions + a name = buy. Shrug at pain + disclosure = no.
  "Send me info" = polite no → get the reason + a referral.
- **Ask once:** two or three slots, free because they do the work, authorship stays
  theirs. Then always: "who else should I learn from?"
- **Within 10 min:** write the **five answers**.
