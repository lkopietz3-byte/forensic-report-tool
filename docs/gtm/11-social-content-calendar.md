### POST 1 — Thought leadership: the pattern in struck declarations
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: pattern hook*

Every struck expert declaration I've read from 2025–2026 has the same thing in common.

It isn't that the expert used AI.

It's that the tool introduced something the expert didn't supply and didn't catch — and the expert couldn't account for what the tool did.

In *Kohls v. Ellison* (D. Minn., Jan. 2025), a Stanford misinformation scholar let GPT-4o fill in citations. It fabricated two articles and misattributed a third. The court struck the entire declaration.

In *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal., May 2025), a data scientist's declaration cited a real journal — correct volume, page, year — but with a title and authors the AI invented. One paragraph struck; court drew the line plainly: there is "a world of difference between a missed citation and a hallucination generated by AI."

The pattern isn't "AI is dangerous." The pattern is: AI in a role the expert wasn't supervising, introducing something the expert couldn't verify.

That's a methodology problem. It has a methodology answer.

### POST 2 — Thought leadership: the safe-harbor case
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: contrast hook*

One expert got their testimony allowed. Another got struck. Same technology, different outcome.

In *Ferlito v. Harbor Freight Tools* (E.D.N.Y., Apr. 2025), a long-experienced expert drafted his report independently and used AI only to confirm conclusions he had already reached on his own.

The court allowed the testimony.

The difference wasn't whether AI touched the work. It was where the expert's judgment sat relative to what the tool did. The expert was in the chair. The AI was in a supporting role he could account for.

That's the safe-harbor pattern every case I've read is pointing toward:

— AI as an assistant the expert supervises and can verify
— The expert's own analysis driving the opinion
— The expert able to stand behind every word

### POST 4 — Caselaw education: CLF v. Shell
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: rule-first hook*

Rule 26(a)(2)(B) already requires an expert to disclose the facts or data they considered. Courts are starting to read AI prompts as part of that.

In *Conservation Law Foundation v. Shell Oil* (D. Conn.), a magistrate judge ordered an expert to produce the AI prompts she used to narrow a large document production — holding that the process was "part of that methodology and therefore discoverable."

A few things to know about that ruling before you draw conclusions:

1. It is a magistrate decision. It has been objected to under Rule 72(a) and is stayed pending the district court's review. It is not settled law.

2. The principle behind it is not new. An expert's methodology has always been fair game. What is new is the recognition that how you prompted an AI tool can be part of that methodology.

3. The practical implication: if your AI interactions are potentially discoverable, keeping them in a form you can produce — contemporaneously, not reconstructed — is better than not.

That's not fearmongering. It's the same standard you'd apply to any methodology step.

### POST 5 — Thought leadership: Rule 702 + AI
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: rule-first hook*

Amended Rule 702 (effective December 1, 2023) requires the party offering expert testimony to show — not just assert — that the opinion rests on sufficient facts or data and reflects a reliable application of reliable methods.

The burden is on the proponent.

Add AI into the workflow and that burden gets more complicated in one specific way: if the AI tool contributed to the analysis, the question "what facts or data did you rely on?" now includes what the tool was given and what it returned.

An expert who can show their work — who has a contemporaneous record of exactly what the tool was given for each section and what it produced — is in a very different position from one who has to reconstruct it months later when a motion to exclude lands.

This isn't a new rule. It's the old rule applied to a new kind of methodological step.

### POST 6 — Build-in-public: the closed-world rule
*Type: Build-in-public | Hook: builder note hook*

The single hardest design decision in building Disclosed.:

The tool should be *unable* to introduce a fact, figure, or citation the expert didn't supply — not just disallowed, but architecturally blocked.

That's what I call the closed-world rule. Every factual sentence in the structured output has to carry a citation marker that resolves to a source unit the expert actually provided. If the sentence can't be traced, the tool flags it as a gap and blocks export until the expert resolves it.

No filling in. No best-guess citation. A gap.

That's a harder constraint to build than a content filter. It's also the only thing that makes the disclosure record meaningful. If the tool can introduce what you didn't give it, the disclosure appendix can't reliably tell opposing counsel what evidence the AI actually used.

The gap-and-flag behavior is not a guardrail. It's the product.

### POST 7 — Thought leadership: disclosure is the fix
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: pattern hook*

The courts striking expert declarations in 2025–2026 are not saying AI is off-limits. ABA Formal Opinion 512 confirmed that AI assistance is permitted with competence and disclosure.

The cases keep pointing to the same two things:

**Competence** — The expert's own judgment drives the opinion. AI is in a supporting role the expert supervises and can verify. The tool doesn't introduce something the expert didn't supply or can't account for.

**Disclosure** — If AI was used, the expert can explain how. What tool, what version, what it was given, what it returned.

Neither of these requires avoiding AI. Both require using it in a way you can account for.

A methodology record that's built contemporaneously — not reconstructed when a deposition question surfaces it — is what makes that possible.

### POST 9 — Thought leadership: the tamper-evident record
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: question hook*

If opposing counsel asked you right now to produce every AI prompt you used in your last report — every input you gave the tool, every section it touched — what would you hand them?

For most experts working with AI tools today, the honest answer is: notes, maybe. A memory of what you did. Nothing contemporaneous and verifiable.

*Conservation Law Foundation v. Shell Oil* suggests that's the kind of thing a court might ask for. (Reminder: that's a non-final magistrate order under Rule 72(a) review — signal of direction, not settled law.)

The methodology answer isn't to avoid AI. It's to run AI in a workflow that keeps a record built for exactly that question.

What that record needs to contain: which sections the tool touched, which model and version, what evidence you gave it — and nothing you did not give it. Contemporaneous. Producible. Structured.

### POST 10 — Build-in-public: the tamper-evident design decision
*Type: Build-in-public | Hook: builder note hook*

One of the more interesting design decisions in building Disclosed.: the disclosure log is append-only and tamper-evident, not tamper-proof.

Tamper-proof would be a stronger claim. It would also be false. The record is stored; it can be altered. What the design does is make alteration *detectable*: each entry is cryptographically linked to the one before it, so a deletion or modification breaks the chain and is visible on load.

I use "tamper-evident" deliberately because "tamper-proof" is the kind of word a cross-examining attorney could dismantle in two questions. Saying what the system actually does — detects alteration rather than prevents it — is both more honest and more defensible under the kind of scrutiny this audience faces every day.

The whole product is built on that discipline. What the tool does is what we say it does, not a better-sounding version of it.

### POST 11 — Thought leadership: recapping the arc
*Type: Thought leadership | Hook: pattern hook*

The arc of AI and expert witnesses from 2024 to mid-2026, as plainly as I can state it:

**2024:** *Matter of Weber* (N.Y. Sur. Ct.) — expert used AI to "check" valuation math, couldn't explain the tool or recall the prompts. Valuation found unreliable.

**Jan. 2025:** *Kohls v. Ellison* (D. Minn.) — AI-fabricated citations; full declaration struck.

**May 2025:** *Concord Music Group v. Anthropic* (N.D. Cal.) — AI-invented authors on a real journal; paragraph struck; court: "a world of difference between a missed citation and a hallucination."

**Apr. 2025:** *Ferlito v. Harbor Freight* (E.D.N.Y.) — expert used AI only to confirm already-reached conclusions; testimony allowed.

**2026:** *CLF v. Shell* (D. Conn.) — magistrate orders expert to produce AI prompts as discoverable methodology. [Non-final; under Rule 72(a) review; stayed pending district court.]

No uniform expert-AI disclosure rule yet. But the direction is not ambiguous. The experts who are fine kept their judgment in the chair and could show their work.
